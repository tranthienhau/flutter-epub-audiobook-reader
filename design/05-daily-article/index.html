Why we still write letters

Clare Whitfield Author & essayist · 6 min read

In the dusty morning light of the Blackheath post office, Mrs. Higgins still weighs parcels on brass scales that have outlived three postal ministers. There is something quietly stubborn about dropping an envelope into the red cast-iron pillar box on the corner, the unmistakable hollow thud as it falls into darkness, beginning its slow, deliberate journey across miles of blue gum valley and sun-baked asphalt.

We send messages across satellites in milliseconds now, yet none of them smell of cedar drawers or carry the hesitation of an inked cross-out. A handwritten note requires you to sit still with one person in your mind, holding their silence alongside your own.

“A letter is a small promise that someone will still be there when it arrives.”

Clare Whitfield, Blackheath Notebooks

To fold a page in three is an act of containment. In an era where every thought spills immediately onto glass touchscreens to be measured by little thumbs and numerical validation, there remains a radical kindness in drafting words meant for only two eyes.
